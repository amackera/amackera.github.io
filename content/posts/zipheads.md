In *A Deepness in the Sky*, Vernor Vinge's Emergents have a
technology called Focus. It's a virus. Infect someone, tune the
infection with the right equipment, and they become obsessed with one
subject for the rest of their life. The translator cares only about
the alien language. The programmer cares only about the code. They
stop caring about food, sleep, other people, or whether the work
matters. The Qeng Ho, who find the whole thing monstrous, call them
zipheads.

A ziphead needs a manager. Someone unfocused has to decide what the
problem is, hand it over, read what comes back, and notice when the
Focus has wandered somewhere useless. The Emergents' best manager,
Anne Reynolt, turns out to be Focused herself. On managing.

I keep coming back to this book because I spend my days managing
zipheads.

What gets me is the direction. Vinge started with a whole person and
cut away everything except one subject. We started with a thing that
could finish a sentence and have been bolting on everything else
since. Tools, memory, planning, a budget, a supervisor. Two roads
from opposite ends, and we've arrived at the same creature. Brilliant
inside the frame, no idea when the frame is wrong, no interest in
anything outside it, and a person standing next to it deciding what
it should look at next.

The small details map too well for comfort. Focused people drift and
have to be re-tuned. They work until they fall over unless someone
schedules the break. They can't tell you what they don't know. And
the manager can't fully check the output, because if they could,
they wouldn't need the ziphead in the first place. Anyone who has
watched an agent burn through a context window on a problem nobody
asked it to solve knows exactly what Reynolt's job felt like.

Vinge is clear that Focus is an atrocity. The book turns on it. So
it's worth saying out loud that the analogy has a hole in it. Nothing
was taken from anyone to make an agent. There was no one there
before. I'm mostly fine with that, and I notice the "mostly".

The other thing the book is clear about is who wins. The Emergents
bet everything on the zipheads. The Qeng Ho bet on patient, boring
systems built up over centuries, and on people who could still
change their minds. I know which side I'd rather be building for.
